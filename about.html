From practice room to ballroom

I started at seven on a quarter-size violin my grandmother found in a charity shop in Norwich. By sixteen I was travelling to London every Saturday for junior department lessons, and at eighteen I took a place at the Royal College of Music, where I studied performance and spent four years learning how to hold a phrase together when your hands are cold and the hall is full.

Conservatory teaches technique. Working life teaches everything else. After graduating I played in pit orchestras, deputised for chamber groups, and took every hotel residency I could get. Two winters of five nights a week in a lobby lounge will teach you how to read a room faster than any masterclass: when to lift the volume, when to pull right back so two people can finish a conversation, when a table of guests wants Piazzolla instead of Bach.

These days my diary is mostly weddings, gala dinners and corporate receptions, with a standing residency in a Mayfair hotel lounge through the autumn. I play solo most often, and bring a cellist or a second violin when the room and the budget call for it.

How I work with you

01

The conversation

A call or a coffee, usually twenty minutes. Venue, timings, the pieces you already have in your head, and the ones you thought you couldn't ask for.

02

The arrangement

If a song you love has no solo violin part, I write one. First dances, family hymns and a surprising amount of film music get arranged from scratch each season.

03

The run of show

I send a timed sheet to you and your planner: processional, register signing, recessional, drinks hour. Your coordinator gets my mobile on the day.

04

The day itself

I arrive ninety minutes early, in black, with my own amplification for larger rooms and a battery unit for gardens with no power.
